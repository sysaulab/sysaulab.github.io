An Essay on UFOs and God

What are they?

On the phenomenon that refuses a single answer, the control system that shapes belief, and the theological question hiding behind the flying saucer. Not a story about one visitor — a story about a category that keeps breaking.

The question that will not sit still

“What are they?” sounds like a simple question. It is not. It assumes that UFOs are one kind of thing — objects, probably spacecraft, probably from somewhere else. But the reports do not behave like a single kind of thing. They behave like a category error that keeps reappearing in different costumes.

Sometimes they are lights in the sky. Sometimes they are apparitions. Sometimes they are encounters with beings who seem more like spirits than astronauts. Sometimes they are psychological events. Sometimes they are all of these at once, depending on who is telling the story and what vocabulary is available.

The honest answer is: nobody knows. And that uncertainty is not a failure of research. It is the shape of the problem. The phenomenon is not just unexplained. It is unexplained in a way that keeps changing the rules of explanation.

The comforting myth is that there is one answer. Either they are extraterrestrials, or they are nothing, or they are demons, or they are angels. But the history of the phenomenon suggests something less tidy: that “UFO” is a label we put on a boundary where our categories fail — and that the label itself may be part of what the phenomenon does.

This is where Jacques Vallée’s work becomes useful. Not because he solved the mystery, but because he changed the question.

The control system

Vallée’s “control system” theory is a natural starting point because it already dissolves the usual conflict. In his view, UFOs are not spacecraft from another planet. They are a consciousness-shaping mechanism — an “intelligent thermostat” from “other dimensions beyond spacetime” that regulates humanity’s collective psyche by manipulating belief, fear, and imagination across history.

The same phenomenon appears as fairies in one era, angels in another, and aliens in ours. The costumes change. The function remains: it shapes what we believe is possible. It does not simply visit us. It adjusts us.

If the phenomenon is a control system, the question shifts from “Are they from Zeta Reticuli?” to something stranger: Who or what is running the thermostat, and toward what end? That is where the theological reconciliations begin.

The control system does not ask us to believe in aliens. It asks us to notice that the phenomenon behaves like a regulator of belief itself — and that the question of its source is inseparable from the question of what we are for.

Frameworks that hold God and aliens together

Across history and across traditions, the reconciliation happens by redefining one of the terms. Either the “aliens” are not biological extraterrestrials, or “God” is not a separate supernatural agent, or the “location” of the phenomenon is shifted. Here are the main approaches.

Psychological

Archetype and trickster

Jungian readings treat UFOs as manifestations of deep psychic structures — a newly dominant archetype, often the trickster, that blurs inner and outer, material and immaterial. The alien becomes a modern mask for a numinous force.

Spiritual

Demonic deception

A dominant conservative Christian framework: UFOs are real, but their occupants are demonic entities masquerading as extraterrestrials. The purpose is theological deception, and the “aliens” are fallen spiritual beings within a biblical cosmology.

Islamic

The jinn framework

Jinn are sentient, non-human creations of Allah, made of smokeless fire, capable of appearing in unusual forms. Some Islamic answers suggest UFO phenomena could be jinn manifestations. Belief in them is not required, but the category absorbs the question.

Jewish

Kabbalistic beings

Jewish mystical literature records encounters with angels, demons, and other classes of being. Some contemporary writers compare these to modern alien reports — the old legends and the new legends may describe the same underlying reality under different vocabularies.

Metaphysical

Interdimensional

Rather than ETs traveling across space, UFOs and their occupants originate from higher dimensions or parallel realities that intersect with our own. Some Christian thinkers adopt this language: “aliens” may be interdimensional heavenly creatures.

Technological

Simulation

If reality is a simulation, UFOs might be glitches, programmers, or watchers. The theological question becomes whether there is a God or merely a cosmic coder — and whether “God” and “aliens” are different tiers of intelligence within a nested reality.

Theological

Process and panentheism

Process theology envisions God as the persuasive, cosmic lure toward novelty and complexity within an evolving universe. Extraterrestrial life would be a natural expression of a cosmos alive with creative possibility. Panentheism places any aliens within God’s being.

Religious

UFO religions

Groups like the Aetherius Society and others have built explicit theologies around aliens, often drawing on Theosophy and the idea of Ascended Masters. The aliens become messengers, ascended teachers, or technological angels within a cosmic hierarchy.

Each framework does the same basic move. It refuses to let “alien” and “divine” sit in separate compartments. Either the alien is a spiritual being, or the divine is a cosmic intelligence, or the phenomenon is located in a reality where the distinction between the two is not stable.

The common thread

Across all of these frameworks, the reconciliation happens by redefining one of the terms. Either the “aliens” are not biological extraterrestrials — demons, jinn, angels, archetypes, glitches — or “God” is not a separate supernatural agent — process theology, panentheism, simulation — or the “location” of the phenomenon is shifted — interdimensional, psychic.

Vallée’s control system sits at the center of this map because it is deliberately agnostic about the source while insisting on the phenomenon’s reality and its function: it does something to human consciousness.

The theological question is not “Where are they from?” It is “What is the thermostat for, and who set it?” The phenomenon may be a mirror, a test, a deception, a visitation, or a regulator. But in every case, the question of God returns not as a competing explanation, but as the question of what kind of reality could produce such a thing — and what it wants from us.

This is why the debate so often collapses into noise. People argue about whether UFOs are “real” without asking what kind of reality they might belong to. They argue about whether aliens are “spiritual” without asking whether the spiritual is a separate realm or a deeper layer of the same one.

The control system does not settle that argument. It reframes it.

So what are they?

They are what we call the point where our categories fail. They are reports of anomalous objects, entities, and experiences that resist a single ontology. They are a mirror that shows us the vocabulary we have available. They are a thermostat that adjusts what we believe is possible. They are, possibly, visitors. They are, possibly, spirits. They are, possibly, us — or something that uses us.

The label matters less than the function. If the phenomenon shapes belief, then the first question is not “What are they?” but “What are they doing to us?” And the second question is “What does that doing imply about the order of reality in which we live?”

The problem is not that we lack an answer. The problem is that we keep demanding a single answer from a phenomenon that may be designed to defeat single answers. The control system is not a doctrine. It is a warning: stop looking for one villain, one planet, one explanation. Look at the function. Look at the thermostat. Look at what it is regulating.

That is where God and aliens stop being rivals. They become questions about the same thing: whether reality is closed or open, whether consciousness is fundamental or incidental, and whether the strange lights in the sky are a distraction from that question or a way of forcing it on us.

Maybe they are visitors. Maybe they are mirrors. Maybe they are a control system. But the moment we ask what they are, we are already asking what we are — and what, if anything, is above us.

What Are They? — an essay on UFOs, the control system, and the question of God.

Released for use, criticism, and revision. It is a hypothesis, not a doctrine.

This page is a lay essay. It does not claim completeness. It claims only that the phenomenon resists a single answer, that Vallée’s control system reframes the question, and that the reconciliation of God and aliens usually happens by redefining one of the terms.
