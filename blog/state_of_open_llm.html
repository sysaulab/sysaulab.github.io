A Field Guide — 2026

"Open" is doing an enormous amount of unearned work.

For a decade the word "open" in AI meant roughly one thing: you could download the file and run it. In 2026 it means at least three different things, and the people using it rarely say which. Meanwhile the geography has sorted itself with unusual clarity — the American frontier is closed and API-gated, the Chinese frontier is open-weight and strategically distributed, and almost nothing on either side would survive an honest audit against the four freedoms. This guide separates the three meanings, maps the two camps, and applies one test.

What does "open" actually mean?

The open AI conversation is not confused about facts so much as conflated about terms. Three distinct claims travel under the same word, and the slippage between them is where most of the industry's marketing lives. Open weights is a distribution property. Open source is a licensing and reproducibility property. Libre is a freedom property, and it is the strictest of the three.

None of these is a moral failing on its own. The failure is letting a model that passes the first test be described with the vocabulary of the third — and then letting the description stand.

The core tension: a model can be downloadable, modifiable, and redistributable while still being unauditable, unreproducible, and revocable by license. Those are not edge cases. That is the default configuration of nearly every model marketed as open in 2026.

Definition One

Open weights

You can download the parameters and run them on your own hardware. That is the entire claim. The training data is secret, the training code is partial or absent, and reproducibility is a rumor. This is what most "open" releases actually deliver, and it is a genuine good — you can self-host, you can fine-tune, you are not renting.

It is also the weakest of the three claims, and the one most often dressed up as the strongest.

Definition Two

Open source (OSI)

The Open Source AI Definition 1.0, finalized in late 2024, asks for more than weights. It asks for data information — enough detail about the training data that a skilled person could build a substantially equivalent system — along with the code and the parameters.

By this standard, a released checkpoint with a published paper is not open source. It is a well-documented artifact.

Definition Three

Libre

The four freedoms: run the program, study it, redistribute copies, and improve it and release your improvements. Applied to a model, this means the weights, the code, the data information, and a license that does not revoke any of it.

Almost nothing in the current landscape passes. A license with a revenue trigger is not libre. A license with an attribution mandate is not libre. Both are business models wearing open-source clothing.

Why It Matters

The word is the product

"Open" is not a technical descriptor in 2026; it is a market position. It buys goodwill, community contributions, talent, and press coverage. A lab that can secure those benefits while retaining practical control over the model has found the best of both worlds — and the only thing standing in the way is precision about terminology.

Precision is therefore the first casualty. Read the license, not the launch post.

The wall: the American frontier is closed

The American frontier is not "moving toward closed." It is closed. OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google ship API access and nothing else. You cannot download GPT, Claude, or Gemini. You rent them. When the terms change, or the price changes, or the model is deprecated underneath your product, you find out by reading a changelog.

This is a coherent business strategy. It is also a moat, and the industry has become fluent in describing moats as safety features. Both things can be true at once; the problem is when only one of them is said out loud.

The pattern, in one line: closed flagship, open decoy. The frontier stays behind the endpoint; the smaller siblings are donated to the community, where they buy a talent pipeline and a standards position for free.

The Closed Frontier

OpenAI, Anthropic, Google

The three defining American labs all ship closed-weight models behind APIs. There is no download, no self-hosting, no fork. The model is a service, and the service is the product.

This is the cleanest possible statement of the position: the weights are the asset, and the asset does not leave the building.

The Exception

Meta and the Llama lineage

Llama 2 and Llama 3 were the free-world counterweight — flawed licenses, yes, with acceptable-use policies and naming clauses, but weights you could hold. Llama 4 arrived in April 2025 under a community license that is emphatically not open source, and the largest model in the family was never released at all.

The trend line points one way: the flagship stays home, the smaller siblings get donated.

The Honest Holdout

Anthropic

Anthropic has never released an open-weight model. Not a small one, not a research one, not a community edition. The most safety-forward lab in the industry has concluded that the safest place for a model is behind its own endpoint.

Whatever one thinks of the conclusion, it is at least stated without the vocabulary of openness. That consistency is worth noting.

Why It Matters

The endpoint is the contract

When you build on a closed model, the terms of your product are set by someone else's terms of service. Rate limits, deprecations, pricing, and acceptable-use enforcement are not engineering constraints you can design around — they are policy you discover.

The open-weight argument was never primarily about cost. It was about who holds the kill switch.

The garden: China commoditizes the layer below

The garden — PyTorch, Hugging Face, the transformer paper, the whole apparatus of modern machine learning — was largely built by American institutions and then fenced off by American corporations. The open commons is the substrate the closed labs were raised on.

China noticed. And China is now doing to the American frontier what American software did to every other industry in the 1990s: commoditizing the layer below it.

The Hinge

DeepSeek-R1

January 2025. 671 billion parameters, 37 billion active, a genuine reasoning model — and an MIT license. Not "community." Not "research only." MIT, the most permissive license in common use, applied to a frontier-class model, with a technical report attached.

The market reaction was not subtle. The licensing reaction was the real story: a frontier lab had published under terms that permitted anything.

The Slow Win

Qwen

Alibaba's Qwen line has been more consequential, quietly, for longer. Apache 2.0 on most open sizes, a sprawl of variants — code, math, vision, tiny — and a download share on Hugging Face that, by multiple 2025 accounts, overtook the American open-weight field.

Qwen is not winning benchmarks. It is winning defaults. That is infrastructure capture, and it compounds.

The Tell

Kimi K2

Moonshot's Kimi K2, July 2025: a one-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts under a Modified MIT License — free, unless your product crosses a monthly-active-user threshold or a revenue threshold, at which point you must display "Kimi K2" in your interface.

That is not open source. That is advertising, purchased with weights. But the rent is a logo, not a per-token invoice.

Why It Matters

Four cold reasons

This is not altruism. Compute export controls constrain Chinese labs, so distribution is the weapon they actually have. Every dollar of value that drains out of the model layer flows to applications, and they would rather own the base of a huge ecosystem than the top of a small one. Contributors become users, users become advocates, advocates become hires. And openness is national strategy, not corporate accident.

The asymmetry in one sentence: America built the garden, then built walls around it, then put a sign on the wall that says open. China is dancing in the garden. It did not build the garden. It is watering it, though — and it is handing out seeds.

The catch

The "China open, America closed" framing is accurate as far as it goes, and it is also where most threads on this subject stop. The scare quotes around libre never come off. The Chinese releases are more open than the American frontier, and they are still not libre.

Caveat One

The licenses are not libre

Kimi's modified license carries a revenue trigger and an attribution mandate. That is a business model wearing an open-source costume. The OSI would not recognize it, and neither should you.

It is still vastly more permissive than an API. The point is not that it is bad. The point is that it is not what the word "open" is supposed to mean.

Caveat Two

Nobody is reproducible

DeepSeek published a paper. DeepSeek did not publish the data. Under the Open Source AI Definition's own text, that fails the data-information requirement. The weights are open; the recipe is not.

You can run it and you can fork it, but you cannot audit it, and you certainly cannot rebuild it. That is a real difference from Linux, and pretending otherwise is how the word lost its meaning.

Caveat Three

The research labs are the actual movement

The genuinely libre options are neither American frontier nor Chinese frontier. They are AI2's OLMo, which ships weights, data, and code under Apache 2.0, and EleutherAI's Pythia.

That is the actual open-source AI movement, and it is orders of magnitude smaller than the models we argue about.

Caveat Four

"America" is a sloppy unit

Mistral is French and sits in the middle of this spectrum — permissive on small models, proprietary on large ones. If you write "America closed, China open," a European will correctly point out that the most interesting middle ground is theirs.

The useful axis is not nationality. It is license text.

The test that actually matters

Forget benchmark deltas. Forget parameter counts. Ask one question of any model claiming the word "open": can I fork it, and can I prove what is inside?

DeepSeek-R1. Fork yes, prove no. MIT license, technical report, no data information.

Kimi K2. Fork yes, prove no, and you may owe them a logo above a usage threshold.

Qwen. Fork yes, prove no. Apache 2.0, which is the strongest license in the open-weight field and still says nothing about the recipe.

OLMo. Fork yes, prove yes. Weights, data, and code. The only entry that passes both halves.

GPT, Claude, Gemini. Fork no. Which at least has the virtue of honesty.

The scoreboard: the Chinese releases win the fork test and lose the audit test. The American frontier loses both and markets the result as a safety feature. The research labs pass both and cannot afford to compete. Nobody is winning on both axes at frontier scale, and that is the actual state of open AI.

What to watch

Does any top-tier American lab go back to open? Not a 7B. A flagship, MIT or Apache, no monthly-active-user clause. One such release would reset the entire argument. Nothing in the current incentive structure suggests it is coming.

Does the OSI definition get teeth? If "open source AI" becomes a certification with consequences — a badge you can lose — the decoy releases get more expensive. If it stays a press-release adjective, the word is dead and we should all say "open weights" and mean it.

Does the license drift run forward or backward? Watch whether the Chinese licenses get more restrictive as the models get better, or less. Kimi's attribution clause is the tell. If the frontier keeps licensing itself into a soft proprietary regime, "open" was a go-to-market strategy, not a philosophy.

What would make it real: a frontier-class release under an unmodified permissive license, with data information sufficient to rebuild it, and no revocation clause. The pieces exist separately. The combination does not, anywhere, yet.

Verification notes

This guide deliberately excludes specific 2026 model releases, parameter counts, and license changes, because those move faster than any page can be maintained and because several circulating figures are unverifiable. Where a number appears above, it is one of the following.

Solid

Cited figures

The MIT license runs roughly 170 words. DeepSeek-R1 shipped in January 2025 at 671B total / 37B active parameters under an MIT license with a published technical report. Qwen's open releases are predominantly Apache 2.0. Kimi K2 shipped in July 2025 at 1T total / 32B active under a Modified MIT License with usage thresholds. Llama 4 shipped in April 2025 under the Llama 4 Community License, with the largest model unreleased. Anthropic has never published open weights. OLMo and Pythia ship data and code alongside weights.

Check Yourself

Directional claims

The Hugging Face download-share crossover — Chinese open-weight models overtaking American ones — was widely reported in 2025, but the specific figure and date matter enormously to how the claim reads. Pull a primary source before quoting a number.

Excluded

Unverified releases

Any 2026 model name, parameter count, or license change not listed in the "Cited figures" card above is absent on purpose. If you have seen one quoted elsewhere, treat it as unverified until you have the model card in front of you.

The Rule

Read the license

Every claim in this guide reduces to one instruction, and it is not a technical one. The launch post is marketing. The benchmark chart is marketing. The license text is the only document that tells you what you are actually allowed to do.
