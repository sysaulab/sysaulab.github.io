A Lay Guide

Society as a field with a depth, a surface, and a membrane.

A way of seeing the economy and society as one layered medium: things accumulate at depth, signals travel across the surface, and boundaries form where pressures balance. Not a new theory — a map that puts familiar ideas on the same page.

What is a field?

Most maps of society show one thing at a time: income, or votes, or posts, or firms. A field is a different way of drawing the map. Instead of one number, you draw several at once, each showing how something varies from place to place and moment to moment.

A weather map is a field. It shows temperature, pressure, and wind as separate layers over the same ground. No single layer tells you whether a storm is coming. Together they do.

The socioeconomic field is the same idea applied to people and institutions. It is not one measurement. It is a bundle of maps laid on top of each other: who is where, what is moving, what has built up, and what is being said.

The core idea: society behaves like a layered medium. Things accumulate at depth. Signals travel on the surface. Between them, boundaries form — membranes — that decide what gets through, and at what cost.

Why bother with the picture? Because it makes three things visible that a single-number map hides: the difference between what is said and what is stored; the fact that some people's signals travel while others' do not; and the way the boundaries between groups are not fixed walls but balances of pressure that can shift.

The three layers

The field has three parts. They are not separate places. They are three ways of describing the same society, depending on how fast you look and how deep you go.

Layer One

Depth — accumulation

Wealth, debt, savings, trust, skills, infrastructure, laws, reputation, and the memory of past deals. These change slowly. They are the standing stock — how much is already there.

Depth is not a literal direction. It is shorthand for "slow, stored, and hard to see from outside." A neighborhood with deep stocks can absorb a shock. A neighborhood without them cannot, even if the two look identical on the day the shock arrives.

Layer Two

Surface — signaling

Prices, purchases, posts, headlines, applications, complaints, protests, and status displays. Fast, visible, and the easiest thing to measure.

Most of what we call "the economy" or "public opinion" is surface data. The surface is real, but it is thin: it records what got expressed, not what exists. A quiet person and an absent person look the same there.

Layer Three

Membrane — the balance point

Where two regimes press against each other, a boundary forms and holds: formal and informal, creditworthy and unbanked, inside and outside, regulated and unregulated.

The membrane is not a wall. It is a tension equilibrium — a balance of pressures that stays put until one side pushes harder. It decides what crosses — money, people, information, legitimacy — and at what cost.

Why It Matters

The membrane is the interesting part

Most analysis stares at the surface. The membrane is where the action is: it is the filter that turns equal effort into unequal results.

Two people can make the same request and get different answers, not because the requests differ but because the membrane between them and the institution is thinner for one than the other.

The two failure modes. A field can fail from the bottom or the top. Deep without feedback: accumulated power that no longer hears the shore. Surface without depth: constant signaling that can never build anything. Health requires both layers, in tension.

How things move: waves and shallow water

Think of a decision as a wave. A large investor moving capital, a media campaign, a new regulation, a rent increase: each one starts somewhere and travels. How far it goes depends on the medium it crosses.

In deep water — thick markets, strong institutions, available credit, dense networks — a wave travels far and stays coherent. The people who make such waves usually have the depth to do it: they can spend, wait, coordinate, and repeat.

In shallow water — thin margins, weak institutions, fragmented trust, no savings buffer — the same wave slows, steepens, and breaks. When it breaks, the energy does not vanish. It lands on the shore: higher rents, new fees, longer commutes, more paperwork, more volatility.

The key point: "collapsing in shallow water" is not a metaphor for failure. It is a description of where the energy is deposited. Large forces are generated in deep water and dissipated in shallow water, and the two are usually far apart — and occupied by different people.

This is why a policy or a market move can look calm and successful from where it was made, and feel like turbulence where it arrived. The wave maker often never sees the shore.

The unequal surface

The surface is not a fair microphone. Signal strength depends on resources: money to spend, time to organize, lawyers, media access, credit, connections, and the ability to wait for a slow payoff.

Wealthy actors can make long, slow, coherent waves — a decades-long investment thesis, a lobbying campaign, a brand. Poor actors often make short, sharp, quickly damped signals — a missed bill, a shift swap, a local protest, a message to family. Both are signals. Only one is legible to the formal surface.

Abject poverty is not silence. It is high damping and poor connection to the instruments that record.

People without money signal constantly — through mutual aid, migration, informal work, care, refusal, and collective action. But if the surface only records transactions, documents, and credit history, then poverty shows up as absence. That absence is an artifact of the measuring instrument, not a fact about people. "Structurally unheard" is more accurate than "incapable of signaling."

The membrane does the rest. Fees, ID requirements, collateral, stigma, language, distance — each one reflects or absorbs a signal. The same boundary that is nearly transparent for one person is nearly opaque for another.

The escape is coordination. A union, a mutual aid network, a federation of small groups, a shared legal strategy — these raise amplitude and coherence. Ten thousand quiet signals that arrive together are a wave.

Reading the field

The field is a diagnostic picture, not a decision procedure. When something in society seems strange — a policy that should work but doesn't, a community that looks passive but isn't, a crisis that nobody saw coming — the picture suggests a sequence of questions.

  1. Where is the depth? What has accumulated, who holds it, and how fast could it erode?
  2. What does the surface record? What is being counted — and what is invisible because it is not counted?
  3. Where are the membranes? Who pays the toll to cross, and who gets through for free?
  4. Whose waves travel? Trace one decision from where it was made to where it landed.
  5. Where does the energy go? When the wave breaks, who absorbs it?
  6. Is feedback reaching the powerful? Do the people making the waves ever find out what happened on the shore?

If the answers conflict, look at the membrane first. Most surprises live there — in the filter, not in the wave.

A rule of thumb: when the field behaves strangely, suspect the measurement before suspecting the people. What the surface fails to record, the depth still feels.

Old ideas, one diagram

Nothing here is new. The field picture is a way of putting familiar insights on the same page.

Balance sheets are depth. Market prices are surface. "Follow the money" is a field instruction. The study of different forms of capital, of stocks and flows in ecological economics, of centrality in networks, of transaction costs in institutions, of flows and crashes in financial physics — each of these names part of the picture. The field framing does not replace them. It shows how they interact.

The value of the picture is not that it is clever. It is that it keeps three questions in view at once: what is stored, what is signaled, and what is filtered.

What this picture is — and what it is not

It is not a single number. Any index that collapses the field into one score — a wellbeing index, a social capital score — has to decide how to weigh things. That weighing is a value judgment dressed as arithmetic.

It is not a literal physics claim. Society is not a fluid. People interpret, organize, refuse, and change the rules. The field language is a disciplined metaphor, useful only if it earns its keep by making predictions that can be checked.

It is not deterministic, and it is not an excuse. Saying "the wave broke on the shore" does not absolve the wave maker. Power that generates large effects is exactly where responsibility sits.

It is not neutral. The surface is an instrument. What it records is a choice, and that choice has winners. Treating "unmeasured" as "nonexistent" is the central error this picture is meant to prevent.

What would make it real: a clear map from concepts to measurements, stated rules for how the layers interact, boundary conditions, and out-of-sample checks. Without those, it is a good picture. With them, it becomes a model.

In the end, the field is most useful not when it gives a clean answer, but when it makes visible the gap between what is stored and what is said — and the membrane that keeps the two apart.
