Systems Analysis — Wildwood Village

Density in. Waves out.

Space is the only multiplier that matters. Write your defense as a geometric barrier, and let the sheer volume of fire do the work an Aura was never going to do anyway.

What is Villagers?

Wildwood Village is a survival tower-defense game. You build a base, gather resources, and defend against waves of enemies. You press Space to gather. You build towers.

It exists because tower-defense games have spent twenty years adding complex aura systems and synergistic buffs, forgetting the one thing that actually survives the exponential scaling of a 100-wave run: the enemy should die before it reaches the wall.

The core idea: a defense is not a central fortress. It is a perimeter. Space efficiency beats percentage multipliers. A dense ring of towers on the spawn point is worth more than a perfectly buffed core sitting in the middle of the map.

There are no Auras in the maximalist endgame. There are no Quarries to extend range, no Lumber Camps to boost damage. There is pure, unadulterated density. The constraint is the weapon.

Why it exists

Most tower-defense games have no concept of "scarcity shifts." They hand you resources at a steady pace and let you build until the map is full. They tell a lie with an economic curve, and it costs you the run at Wave 70.

Villagers has the concept. The economy is a moving bottleneck. You start starving for Wood. Then Stone becomes the wall. And finally, the Food/Attrition Crisis hits. Because Farms and player regeneration consume Food per HP healed, massive waves turn your food supply into a literal combat health pool. A single bad wave at 97 can drain 6,500 Food and collapse your economy through starvation.

The point is not to survive the wave. The point is to take so little structural damage that your farms never have to heal anything. To commit to a structure before you touch a single buff. If your kill zones don't make sense as geometry, they won't make sense as a defense, and no Aura will save them.

How it works

The math is brutally simple. It requires four buff buildings to double (2x) the power of a single tower. But if you replace those four buff buildings with four additional towers, you get a 5x increase in total firepower (the original tower plus four new ones).

The rules

  • Pods form a perimeter. Each cluster of towers overlaps to create a continuous kill zone. If there is a gap, the wave will find it.
  • A buff building is a trap. It consumes physical tiles that could be used for more towers. The math is unforgiving.
  • Enemies spawn in a ring roughly 520–700 pixels from the Town Center. Build directly on top of it to deny them movement.
  • Overlapping fire creates a shotgun effect, saturating enemies with crossfire the moment they enter the radius.
  • Auras remain viable in early-game resource crunches or incomplete outer pods, but they are mathematically inferior in the late game.

Where Auras fit

Despite the late-game dominance of density, Auras remain strategically viable in two specific scenarios: Early Hard Mode, when resources are too constrained to build a full 5-tower pod, and Incomplete Periphery Pods, acting as a temporary force multiplier until the full tower block is constructed.

Space efficiency is free. By replacing aura buildings with densely packed Lv4 Towers, players maximize their damage-per-tile. The constraint of the map becomes the multiplier.

The Strategy

Traditional defensive play — turtling around a central core — eventually fails under the weight of massive late-game spawns. The optimal strategic configuration shifts to proactive area denial.

Phase Action
Spawn-Camping Construct dense tower pods at the periphery, right on the spawn ring, so enemies aggro instantly upon spawning.
Wave Splitting A central base allows waves to converge into a single unkillable deathball. Perimeter pods split the wave into smaller, manageable engagements.
Ablative Armor Place non-defensive structures (Houses, Storages) at the extreme edges of the map to absorb aggro and buy time for the kill zones.
Player Body-Block Stand in the gaps. With the Aegis (62% reduction), you tank the brutes while the towers behind you shoot through you.

Enemies that overflow the perimeter are auto-aggroed so new spawns are always visible. Manual body-blocking is there for the player when a gap runs long.

The Engine Limits

There is a Wave 150 threshold. The game's difficulty ceiling is not exclusively dictated by game math, but by the rendering and logic engine.

The collision detection function (separateEnemies()) calculates distance checks for every enemy against every other enemy. At 1,500+ enemies, this O(N²) complexity causes severe CPU strain. A* pathfinding algorithms become exponentially heavier as the grid fills with units and structures.

The Math

Exponential scaling

Enemy HP scales at 1 + (waveNum * 0.12), and damage at 1 + (waveNum * 0.05). By Wave 150, a Brute carries 1,600 HP and hits for 110 damage per swing. The mathematical difficulty scales indefinitely.

The Engine

Frame-rate collapse

High-level stress testing reveals that the theoretical mathematical difficulty is eclipsed by a hard hardware limit around Wave 150 due to collision checks and floating damage text rendering.

A note on documentation: The theoretical "infinite" scaling hits a "practical" wall at Wave 100–150. For game developers, the lesson is clear: balance your economic curves, encourage spatial thinking, but ensure your collision detection algorithm is O(N log N) before you let players spawn 200 enemies at once.

Archaeology

The evolution of the maximalist strategy went through several distinct phases. The first two are worth recording, because the difference between them and the final solution is the whole point.

The Turtle

The first. A central fortress. Massive walls. Let them come to you. It absorbed damage and hoped the DPS was high enough before the core fell. It worked, and it was forgotten, by Wave 69 when the core finally collapsed under the weight of the deathball.

The Saturation

The second. The map is physically full. The economy is maxed. The auras are overlapping to the point of diminishing returns. You have hit the practical wall of infrastructure. A private language of defense dies with the mood that invented it.

Villagers

This one. It replaced the Auras with pure density, deleted every buff building in the center, and added exactly one thing the predecessors never had: a perimeter. Pod { towers, farms }. Everything else is a rounding of what was already there.

The first two were built on a laptop during a long weekend, half a drink in, annoyed at the exponential scaling of Wave 70. That is the whole origin story. It is not a dramatic one. It is, on reflection, the correct one.

Design Notes

Timing

The wave takes 5 seconds to wipe. The enemies spend their entire lifespan walking toward the nearest tower, but they die before they ever get in melee range. These numbers are not tuned to be noticed. They are tuned to be finished before the player starts worrying about the next wave.

State

The engine holds a dense grid of towers and a massive array of enemies. Each pod carries its own aggro radius and the sheer list of entities. Animating the crossfire is what makes persistence free. Enemies were never allowed to move, so they never needed to be kited.

Wrapping

The wave wraps around the perimeter. No mid-wave breaks. The player is the one exception, and only when a brute needs to be kited away from a collapsing gap.
