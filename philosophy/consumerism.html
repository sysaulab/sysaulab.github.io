A Curriculum in Indoctrination

How to train a consumer.

A satirical syllabus in ten modules. The goal is not to make anyone buy anything in particular. The goal is to make buying the way a person meets the world. The training is complete when life and consumption become the same word.

Before the price tag

Training does not begin at the checkout. It begins at the pacifier.

Before the child can read, before the child can walk, the child learns the first lesson: discomfort has a remedy, and the remedy is an object. The screen, the toy, the snack, the new thing to hold. The lesson is repeated until it becomes a reflex, and the reflex is repeated until it becomes a personality.

Then come the rituals. The birthday haul. The reward for good behaviour. The supermarket checkout, where the child learns that patience is a small tax and that the tax can be paid in sugar. Love is expressed materially, because the adults have already been trained, and the adults are the first curriculum.

By the time a child can read a price tag, they have already been taught that wanting has a shape, and that the shape is a package.

Grade, rank, and the first ledger

School arrives and formalizes what the home began. Gold stars. Class rank. Rubrics. Bells that divide the day into units of compliance. The child learns that worth is external, numerical, and scarce — that there are winners and losers, and that the difference is printed on a page.

This is the first ledger. It teaches the child to think of themselves as a number that can go up, and to experience going up as the whole point of the day.

Then the working world picks up the same ledger and changes only the font. Pay grades. Performance reviews. Job titles. Career ladders. The ranking feature persists. The gold star becomes a bonus. The report card becomes a résumé. The authority figure moves from the teacher to the boss, but the posture is identical: wait to be evaluated, perform for the evaluation, and treat the evaluation as the truth about yourself.

The report card is the first credit score. The child learns that they are a number that can go up, and that going up is the whole point.

Manufacturing the want

Ranking tells the consumer how to feel about themselves. Advertising tells them what to do about it.

Advertising is not a step in the curriculum; it is the atmosphere in which the curriculum is taught. It teaches that brands are tribes, that purchases are personality, and that envy is a shopping list. It speaks through billboards and feeds, through influencers and unboxings, through the quiet liturgy of treat yourself.

Peer status enforces the lesson. The vacation, the renovation, the new car, the “smart” purchase — these are not merely enjoyed; they are displayed, and the display is graded. To consume is to belong. To abstain is to be strange, or poor, or both.

The culture rewards the behaviour of spending the “well-earned” money, and then calls the reward a free choice.

The trained consumer does not need to be told to buy. They need only to be shown what they are missing, and to be assured that everyone else already has it.

The future on layaway

Here is the step most accounts of consumer training skip, and it is the one that makes the rest compulsory.

A saver with a shopping habit is not yet a consumer. A debtor is. Debt converts the future into inventory. Student loans, credit cards, car loans, mortgages, buy-now-pay-later — each one is a claim on a paycheck that has not arrived, and each one makes the paycheck necessary.

The consumer does not spend “well-earned” money. They spend money that has already been promised to someone else, and then they work to keep the promise. The job becomes necessary because of the debt, and the debt becomes bearable because of the job. The loop is not a trap; it is a business model.

Teach them that money is a feeling and credit is a lifestyle. Teach them that the future is a place you can borrow from, and that the present is a place you can furnish.

The saver is a tourist in the market. The debtor is a resident.

The cage with points

Once the consumer is ranked, advertised to, and in debt, the system adds a scoreboard. Loyalty points. Credit scores. Ratings. Streaks. Subscriptions. Wellness metrics. The consumer is invited to optimize, and optimization feels like agency.

They are winning a game whose rules they did not write, and the winning is the leash.

Points

Loyalty

Spend to earn the right to spend. The reward for consumption is a discount on more consumption.

Scores

Credit

A number that decides what kind of life you are allowed to finance. Improve it by borrowing responsibly, which is to say, by borrowing.

Ratings

Reviews

Everyone is a customer and everyone is a product. Rate the driver, rate the restaurant, rate yourself.

Streaks

Habits

Continuity is the product. Miss a day and lose the identity you bought.

Then the architecture. Convenience is a curriculum. Cars over transit, disposability over repair, subscriptions over ownership. The path of least resistance is paved, and the pavement is the lesson. Waste and labour are hidden behind distance, and the hiding is called efficiency.

Recycling arrives as absolution. The consumer sorts their guilt into the correct bin and feels that the system has been addressed.

Friction is the teacher no one notices. The consumer is trained by what is easy, and what is easy has been chosen for them.

“It’s just a job”

The training must also sever the connection between what a person does and what a person buys. The salary is a moral solvent. It dissolves the link between the occupation and the money received.

The worker learns to compartmentalize. The harm is in another department, another time zone, another line of the spreadsheet. The paycheck is clean because it is spent on clean things: the family, the home, the holiday.

Then moral licensing completes the circuit. Buy green. Donate at checkout. Recycle. Offset the flight. The consumer is issued a small certificate of virtue with every purchase, and the certificate is accepted as payment for the world.

The salary is a moral solvent. It dissolves the connection between what you do for a living and what you are.

Train the next trainer

The final mechanism is not a mechanism at all. It is the family.

Parents who were trained teach children to rank, to reward, to spend, and to rationalize. The birthday haul returns. The gold star returns. The checkout impulse returns. The child learns the syllabus from people who never knew they were teaching it.

The system does not need a conspiracy. It needs a syllabus and a calendar, and it has both.

No one has to enforce the curriculum. It reproduces itself at the dinner table.

Life itself

Then comes graduation. The consumer no longer experiences consumption as one activity among many. It becomes the grammar of existence.

The calendar is colonized. Birthdays, holidays, grief, romance, fitness, parenthood, patriotism — each becomes a shopping occasion or a branded experience.

The language is colonized. “How are you?” becomes “What have you acquired?” “Self-care” becomes retail therapy. “Community” becomes a subscription tier. “Freedom” becomes a choice among packages.

The milestones are colonized. Education is a product. Dating is a marketplace. Health is customer service. Politics is brand loyalty. Death is a premium funeral package.

Time is colonized. Leisure is not rest but planned spending. Burnout is cured by a purchase. The vacation is proof of consumption.

Emotion is colonized. Discomfort is solved by acquisition. Envy is a shopping list. Love is materially expressed. Guilt is absolved at checkout.

Citizenship is colonized. Voting becomes consumer preference. Activism becomes ethical shopping. Sustainability becomes a premium add-on.

And resistance is colonized. Minimalism, anti-consumerism, slow living, and wellness become markets. The system trains you to consume your own critique.

The trained consumer does not choose consumerism among other options. Consumerism is the grammar of choosing. Life is not lived and then consumed — it is consumed as the condition of being lived.

Training is complete when the question “What do you want to do with your life?” is answered with “What do you want to buy?”

The invoice

There is a bill, and it is ecological. Overshoot is the material invoice for the training. The atmosphere does not care about the scoreboard.

But the curriculum has arranged for the invoice to arrive in a form the consumer cannot read as a contradiction. Limits appear as personal inconvenience, or as a bad shopping choice, or as a weather report. The bill is externalized: pushed into distance, into the future, into the ocean, into someone else’s neighbourhood.

And the remedies on offer are products. The reusable cup. The carbon offset. The ethical upgrade. The consumer is invited to shop their way out of the consequences of shopping, and the invitation is itself a market segment.

The bill arrives not as a demand but as a weather report — and the weather is not a customer.

Transparent to itself

Here is the tragedy, and it is not the tragedy of ignorance.

The culture is not opaque because it hides things. It is opaque because it is the medium through which everything is seen. Consumerism is not an object in the field of view; it is the glass. We do not see it. We see through it.

This is what it means for a culture to be transparent to itself: it cannot catch itself in the act of being itself. There is no outside vantage point from which to inspect the grammar, because the grammar is what makes inspection intelligible. To question consumption is already to consume the question.

The culture knows. It has the climate data, the supply-chain maps, the ethical labels, the documentaries. It is informed, guilty, and immobile. Awareness has been absorbed as a product line: the sustainable brand, the anti-consumerist bestseller, the slow-living retreat, the climate-anxiety app.

And so the critique is recuperated. The system does not merely tolerate dissent; it stocks it. The exit is for sale, and the exit sign is a brand.

We do not need to raise awareness. Awareness is already a market segment.

The trap is not that the consumer is deceived. The trap is that the consumer is lucid. They can describe the machine, name its parts, and still reach for the card, because the only available language of change is the language of consumption.

A window is transparent: you do not see the glass, you see through it. So it is with consumerism. It is not hidden from us; it is the thing we look through. And you cannot boycott the glass.

The tragedy is not that the consumer is deceived. It is that the culture is transparent to itself — aware, informed, and still unable to change — because the only available language of change is the language of consumption. Fixing ecological overshoot requires changing a culture that has no outside, and whose critique is already for sale.

The training is complete. The graduate is free — to choose among packages.

How to Train a Consumer — a satirical syllabus on the making of the modern consumer.

Released for use, criticism, and revision. It is a hypothesis, not a doctrine.

This page is a lay essay. It does not claim completeness. It claims only that the consumer is trained long before the first purchase, that debt makes the training compulsory, and that the culture which trains them is transparent to itself — which is to say, it cannot see the glass it looks through.
