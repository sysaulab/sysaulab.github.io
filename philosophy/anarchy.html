An Essay on Autonomy, Command, and the Freedom to Arrive Differently

Any absolute -ism is a failure of the same kind.

This essay starts with a drill maneuver I made when I was in the cadets — twenty feet of error, forty steps of count, a cloud of individual paths, and a captain who had a small heart attack before he applauded. It is about what anarchism actually is (anti-hierarchism, not free-for-all-ism), where it serves a purpose, and why absolute anarchism is no better than any other absolutism. It is about the seam between order and chaos: riding the flexibility while maintaining enough structure.

When I was in the cadets

When I was in the cadets, we made a drill maneuver that now makes me think about anarchy. Let me tell you about it, because the maneuver itself was small and stupid — twenty feet of error, forty steps of count — and it turned out to be one of the cleanest lessons in political philosophy I ever got, delivered by accident, on a parade square, with dice.

We were the peloton de précision, the display drill group. Our job was choreography: fixed formations, fixed counts, music cues, a captain in the reviewing stand. Somewhere in the sequence, the officer in charge realized the group would end up twenty feet too far to the left. A simple translation — everyone shuffle left, keep the lines — would have been correct and unbearable. It would also have been an admission that the plan had failed and the only remedy was a visible patch. I proposed something else: explode the group, let each of us decide how to use the allotted forty steps to reach the final destination, and re-form on the count. A cloud of movements instead of a rigid shift. We used dice to assign the paths. The count held the frame.

The maneuver: the group did not defect. It refused only the boring, rigid way of getting there. The cloud of movements was distributed autonomy: each of us had our own path, assigned by chance, executed on count, landing together.

It worked. The captain had a small heart attack watching the formation dissolve, then realized what we were doing, and applauded. The twenty feet were corrected. The count was respected. And for forty steps, we were free — not because we had defected from the hierarchy, but because we had exercised autonomy inside it, as a method, for a purpose.

What anarchy really is

Anarchy is not a free-for-all. It is not the absence of all order, and it is not chaos. Anarchy, properly understood, is anti-hierarchism: the opposition to unjust, arbitrary, or unnecessary hierarchy, and the insistence that people can organize themselves without being commanded from above. It is not the rejection of structure. It is the rejection of domination. The distinction matters, because it separates anarchy as a practical method from anarchy as an absolutist ideology — and the second is no better than any other absolutism.

Anarchy begins with a question about legitimacy: no one has the right to rule another without justification. Hierarchy is not automatically illegitimate, but it must earn its place. A surgeon directing a trauma team is a hierarchy; a bureaucrat blocking a decision they do not understand is not. Anarchy asks why this hierarchy, serving what purpose, accountable to whom? — and refuses to accept "because it is the hierarchy" as an answer.

The distinction: a free-for-all is not anti-hierarchical; it is simply a different distribution of power, often one in which the loudest or strongest dominate. Anarchy is about distributed autonomy within structures that people actually consent to and can actually use.

This is why anarchy is not free-for-all-ism. It is the practice of finding the seam where freedom serves the purpose — where people exercise initiative inside a frame rather than defecting from it. That is the whole essay in one sentence, and the drill square is its smallest example.

How we used it

On the drill square, we used anarchy exactly that way. The frame was the forty-step count and the final formation. The hierarchy was the officer in charge and the captain in the stand. We did not refuse any of it. We refused only the boring, rigid way of getting there.

The dice were a distributed planning procedure — a way of not needing the officer to choreograph forty steps of cloud. Cage and Cunningham built whole works on chance operations for the same reason: chance generates variety a single planner would not think of, and it distributes decisions instead of centralizing them. The drill-ground version was the same logic in miniature.

Structure

The forty-step count

The constraint was real. We could not add a casual side-step or take extra time. The count held the frame, and the frame made the freedom legible.

Chaos

The dice

Chance-assigned individual paths. Not random noise — a rule plus randomness, executed by the group, landing on time. Distributed planning in its smallest form.

Flexibility

The cloud

Each of us had our own route. The flexibility was trusting everyone to execute their own path and still land together. That trust is what made it a maneuver rather than a mess.

Result

The applause

The captain applauded because the result re-legitimized the system. The heart attack was the moment the command chain briefly lost certainty; the applause was the moment the result restored it.

That is the seam: between obedience and initiative, between following orders and solving the problem the orders were trying to solve. The command chain can absorb that kind of autonomy precisely because it delivers the result. We did not defect. We used our freedom to serve the purpose of the hierarchy — and in doing so, we demonstrated that anarchy can be practiced in many settings, even under drill pressure, even in cadets, even with dice.

The quiet indictment: the officer in charge could not plan the choreography properly, and the group patched over the failure with a rule plus randomness. The applause was for the patch. The heart attack was for the method. Localized anarchy had done the hierarchy's job better than the hierarchy had.

What it has been conflated with

But anarchy has been conflated with something else: absolute anarchism — the insistence on no hierarchy anywhere, always, as an end in itself. That is an absolutism, and it is no better than any other absolutism. Absolute statism says hierarchy always; absolute anarchism says hierarchy never. Both refuse the actual question of legitimacy, purpose, and context. Both substitute a total answer for a practical one.

Absolute anarchism would have demanded the abolition of the count, the formation, and the captain. The parade would have collapsed. The purpose would not have been served; it would have been sacrificed to a principle. That is the failure mode of every absolute -ism: it treats a useful method as a totalizing ideology. It refuses to ask the anarchist's own question — why this hierarchy, serving what purpose? — and instead answers in advance: none. That is as dogmatic as the statist who answers all hierarchy, always.

The correction: any absolute -ism is a failure of the same kind. The problem is not that hierarchy exists. The problem is the total answer — the refusal to ask the contextual question of legitimacy, purpose, and strain.

Absolute anarchism is a total answer to a contextual question. It is not more free than absolute statism; it is the same grammar pointed in the opposite direction. The drill group found the better path: it kept the count, kept the formation, kept the captain, and used the seam between them to be free for forty steps.

Localized anarchy inside a military structure

What the drill square showed is that localized anarchy can be useful, and has proven so even in a military structure. Not because the military is secretly anarchist, but because every command chain has seams — moments when central planning fails, when improvisation is needed, when the people closest to the problem know better than the people giving the orders.

Localized anarchy is the method of using those seams: distribute the decision, trust the group, hold the frame, land on the count. It checks arbitrary authority without overthrowing it. It solves problems the hierarchy cannot solve alone. It is anarchism as a corrective, not as a total system.

Where it serves

When central planning fails

Hierarchies are good at coordination but bad at improvisation. When the choreography drifts twenty feet left, the cloud and the count deliver a result a single planner would never have designed.

Where it serves

When authority becomes arbitrary

The heart attack in the reviewing stand is the sound of a hierarchy briefly losing certainty. The applause is the sound of it recognizing that the result re-legitimized it. Anarchy as method does not need to overthrow the captain; it needs to make the captain's purpose achievable in a freer way.

Where it serves

When decisions need distributing

Chance operations are a legitimate compositional method because they generate variety and distribute decisions instead of centralizing them. The drill-ground version is the same logic in miniature: a rule plus randomness, executed by the group, landing on time.

The pillars are necessary together. Strip any one and you get either determinism, noise, or a stream that looks free without being free. The freedom is real only when the frame is real, the strain is real, and the result lands.

The seam, not the system

So the essay's claim is modest and practical. Anarchism is anti-hierarchism, not free-for-all-ism. It serves a purpose when it is used as a method: to distribute decisions, to improvise when central planning fails, to check arbitrary authority, to let people solve problems the hierarchy cannot solve alone. It does not require defection. It requires finding the seam where autonomy serves the purpose.

But absolute anarchism — the insistence on no hierarchy anywhere, always — is no better than any other absolutism. It is a total answer to a contextual question. The drill group found the better path: they exploded the group, rolled the dice, used the forty steps, and re-formed on the count. The captain had a small heart attack, then applauded. The twenty feet were corrected. The count was respected. And for forty steps, the group was free — not because hierarchy was abolished, but because autonomy was exercised inside the frame, as a method, for a purpose.

The formulation: between order and chaos, riding the flexibility while maintaining enough structure. Anarchism at its most useful is not an absolute, but a seam — a place where a system with something to lose is strained almost to losing it, and holds.

The essay must implicate itself, as its companion did. It is written by someone who found a political philosophy on a parade square and has been suspicious of grand framings ever since — including this one. The temptation is to turn a small, stupid, successful maneuver into a doctrine. The maneuver was not a doctrine. It was a patch, with dice, that worked. The lesson is not that anarchy is the answer. The lesson is that the question — why this hierarchy, serving what purpose, accountable to whom? — is the answer, and that the seam is where the question gets asked in practice.

What has been ruled out before the order is obeyed?
Whose initiative is invisible from where the formation is measured?
What has the frame made possible that the frame could not plan?
Can the group correct its own description of itself?
What happens when the strain is increased?

These questions are not a formula. They are a discipline of attention. They apply to the anarchy narrative, to the structures that carry it, and to the self that receives it — including the self writing this essay. The shared lesson is the same as in the novelty essay: the interesting failures live at the boundaries. The framework is most useful not when it gives a clean answer, but when it makes visible the tension between levels, the blind spots in the nesting, and the strain that is being sustained or lost.

The shared lesson: in anarchy as in novelty, the interesting failures live at the boundaries. Absolute anarchism is the failure mode of treating a seam as a system. Localized anarchy is the discipline of finding the seam and using it — not abolishing the frame, but riding it.

Nothing free ever happens in a system that refuses all structure, and nothing free ever happens in a system that refuses all initiative. Freedom lives in the narrow band where a system with something to lose is strained almost to losing it — and holds. The task is not to dissolve the hierarchy. The task is to learn to look at the seams. That is anarchy in practice. It is the opposite of both the free-for-all and the parade square that never moves.
