A Systematic Ethical Framework

Twenty-six axioms to guide the mind.

A framework for evaluating health at every level of complexity — from cells to ecosystems to future generations. Not a replacement for traditions, but a meta-framework for testing them.

What is this about?

Most ethical frameworks start from a single principle — utility, duty, virtue, care — and derive the rest. This framework starts from a different question: what must a system do to stay healthy? The answer is not one rule but a set of conditions that, taken together, describe the difference between a system that learns, adapts, and persists, and one that runs away, exploits its own substrate, or collapses.

The framework is built on twenty-six axioms. Four are foundational. Twenty are structural. Two are the hinge that binds the levels together. They are not derived from a single master principle. They are a closure — a set of conditions that, together, handle scale, time, power, agency, uncertainty, conflict, and the difference between real and counterfeit complexity.

The core claim: good is the growing diversity of complexity in the world — but not complexity at any cost. It is sustainable, distributed, feedback-rich complexity that does not purchase its growth by simplifying, exploiting, or destroying other systems.

The framework is not a moral algorithm. It does not tell you what to do in every case. It tells you what to check — which questions must be answered before an action can be called healthy, and which failure modes must be ruled out.

The foundation

Four axioms define what a healthy system is. They are not independent — each one constrains the others — but together they establish the baseline against which every other axiom is measured.

  • A1. Feedback integrity. A healthy system must maintain proper feedback loops, to learn from the consequences of its actions.
  • A2. Local exploration. A healthy system must explore its immediate solution space, to maximize its fitness according to its immediate environment.
  • A3. Protection of the fragile. A healthy system must protect the fragile — the opposite of systematic exploitation. One is a runaway process; the other is self-stabilizing.
  • A4. Good as growing complexity. Good is the growing diversity of complexity in the world.

These four are deliberately minimal. They describe a system that can learn (A1), adapt (A2), protect its own conditions of persistence (A3), and increase in richness (A4). Everything that follows is an elaboration of what those four require at scale, over time, and under conditions of power and uncertainty.

Why four and not one? Because a single principle always collapses into one of its failure modes. Feedback without exploration ossifies. Exploration without protection exploits. Protection without growth stagnates. Growth without feedback runs away. The four are a minimal set of mutual constraints.

The structure

Twenty axioms elaborate the foundation. They are grouped here by the dimension of systems health they address — not because the groups are independent, but because the grouping makes the coverage visible.

Scale and Time

A5 - A6

A5. Nested scale. Health must be evaluated at every nested level — individual, community, ecosystem, biosphere, future generations. No subsystem may optimize itself by destroying the larger systems that sustain it.

A6. Multi-timescale. Consequences must be evaluated across immediate, developmental, and evolutionary timescales. Short-term fitness must not foreclose long-term viability.

Feedback and Risk

A7 - A10

A7. Feedback integrity. Feedback must be truthful, timely, causally coupled, and accessible. Suppressing or distorting it is a primary moral harm.

A8. Irreversibility. Risks of irreversible loss require disproportionate justification and precaution.

A9. Option space. Preserve and expand the set of viable futures. Foreclosing options without consent is a moral cost.

A10. Resilience. Protect the fragile, but also build redundancy, diversity, and adaptive capacity.

Power and Justice

A11 - A16

A11. Symbiosis. Relationships should be mutualistic or at least non-parasitic. A system may consume resources, but not destroy the source's capacity to regenerate.

A12. Power accountability. Power must be distributed, checked, transparent, and revocable.

A13. Moral standing. Entities with capacities for suffering, preference, agency, or self-maintenance have moral standing proportional to those capacities.

A14. Consent. Interventions affecting moral agents require consent where possible.

A15. Justice. Benefits and burdens must be distributed fairly. Those made fragile by a system have priority claim on protection and repair.

A16. Responsibility. Moral responsibility scales with causal power, knowledge, freedom, and benefit.

Knowledge and Limits

A17 - A23

A17. Pluralism. Diversity of agents, strategies, and cultures is an epistemic and adaptive resource. Homogenization is a systemic risk.

A18. Truth. Truth-seeking, transparency, and open criticism are moral duties. Deception is a feedback poison.

A19. Stewardship. Present agents hold the world in trust. They may not transfer irreversible harm to future systems.

A20. Subsidiarity. Decisions should be made at the lowest competent level, with affected parties included.

A21. Limits. No subsystem can grow without limit. Complexity growth must respect thermodynamic, material, and ecological constraints.

A22. Fallibilism. All moral rules and institutions are provisional hypotheses. They must remain open to correction.

A23. Anti-counterfeit complexity. Good is not mere complexity. It is complexity that does not purchase its growth by simplifying or destroying other systems.

One further axiom sits between the structure and the hinge:

A24. Conflict resolution. When axioms conflict, default priority is: (1) prevent irreversible collapse and loss of feedback/option space; (2) protect the fragile and non-consenting from exploitation; (3) preserve and distribute agency, consent, and power; (4) increase sustainable, shared diversity of complexity.

The hinge: nested reciprocity

The first twenty-four axioms can still be read as top-down stewardship or bottom-up individualism. They do not, on their own, state the most important structural fact about complex systems: every level owes something to the levels above and below it, and those obligations are reciprocal.

This is the hinge. It is what turns a list of conditions into a nested responsibility ethics.

A25. Nested reciprocity. Every level of complexity exists in mutual dependence with the levels it contains and the levels that contain it. Health requires reciprocal respect: higher levels must preserve the integrity, agency, and conditions of lower levels; lower levels must preserve the conditions and integrity of higher levels. No level may treat another merely as a resource.

A26. Nested responsibility. Responsibility is distributed and bidirectional according to capacity, power, and dependency. The whole is responsible for the parts it constitutes and depends on; the parts are responsible for the wholes they constitute and depend on. For non-agents, this is functional integration. For moral agents, it becomes a moral duty: care, restraint, voice, consent, repair.

The example that makes this concrete is the body. You respect your organs by sleeping, eating well, not poisoning them, seeking medical care. Your organs "respect" you functionally by doing their jobs, maintaining the whole that gives them their environment. But your organs are not moral agents, so they do not have duties in the moral sense. You, as the moral agent, have duties both downward to your parts and upward to your communities, ecosystems, and future generations.

The relation is not symmetrical in capacity, but it is reciprocal in dependency and responsibility. Higher levels often have more power, so they carry more moral responsibility. Lower levels may have less agency, so they are owed more protection.

This avoids two failure modes. The first is tyranny of the whole over the parts — the whole exploits or sacrifices its parts. The second is cancer of the parts over the whole — parts optimize themselves by destroying the larger system. Without A25 and A26, the framework can drift toward one or the other. With them, it holds the tension.

Using the axioms

The axioms are not a decision procedure. They are a checklist for diagnosis. When an action, policy, or institution is proposed, the axioms ask a sequence of questions. The sequence matters, because A24 establishes a default priority when answers conflict.

  1. Does it preserve feedback? Can the system learn from consequences, or is feedback being suppressed, distorted, or monopolized? (A1, A7, A18)
  2. Does it protect the fragile? Is it exploiting a weaker level to benefit a stronger one, or is it self-stabilizing? (A3, A11, A15)
  3. Does it preserve option space? Does it foreclose viable futures, or create irreversible loss? (A8, A9, A19, A21)
  4. Is power accountable? Who decides, who is affected, and can the decision be revised? (A12, A14, A20)
  5. Is the complexity real? Does it grow sustainable, distributed, feedback-rich complexity, or does it purchase growth by simplifying and externalizing disorder? (A4, A17, A23)
  6. Is the relation reciprocal? Does each level respect the conditions that make the other levels possible? (A5, A25, A26)

If the answers conflict, A24 provides the tie-breaker. Irreversible collapse comes first. Protection of the fragile comes second. Agency and consent come third. Growth of sustainable complexity comes fourth. This ordering is not arbitrary: it reflects the conditions that make all the other goods possible.

The framework is fallible by design. A22 says so explicitly. The axioms are hypotheses about what health requires. They are open to correction by feedback, experiment, and affected voices. A framework that could not be revised would violate its own first axiom.

Traditions and axioms

The axioms are not a replacement for traditions. They are a meta-framework: explicit, portable, testable, and fallible. Traditions are embodied, narrative, ritual, communal, and inherited. The axioms ask, "What must a system do to stay healthy?" Traditions answer, "Here is what generations learned when they did or didn't."

The relationship is a loop. Axioms diagnose. They test any tradition or action: Does it preserve feedback? Protect the fragile? Distribute power? Honor nested reciprocity? Avoid irreversible harm? Traditions motivate and transmit. They carry meaning, identity, ritual, emotional regulation, and intergenerational memory. They make abstract principles livable. Feedback corrects both. A tradition without axioms can ossify into dogma. Axioms without tradition can thin into abstraction.

Many traditions already encode this in different languages: covenant, dharma, tao, ubuntu, relationality, stewardship, filial piety, ancestral obligation. They are not identical, and not all parts are wise. The axioms help us compare, prune, repair, and integrate — without pretending we start from zero.

The mind needs principles. The heart needs stories. The system needs feedback. The future needs both.

What this framework is — and what it is not

The framework is careful about its own limits. It is a diagnostic tool, not a moral algorithm. It does not tell you what to do in every case. It tells you what to check, and which failure modes to rule out.

It is not a complete ethics. It does not specify how to weigh competing goods in every context, how to resolve genuine moral dilemmas, or how to cultivate virtue. It is a framework for evaluating system health — not a substitute for practical wisdom, empathy, or lived experience.

It is not a justification for paternalism. A25 and A26 are explicit: higher levels have more power and therefore more responsibility, but lower levels are not mere resources. The framework forbids both tyranny of the whole and cancer of the parts.

It is not a claim to completeness. A22 says the axioms are provisional. A finite set of principles cannot be proven complete. The framework is a closure — complete enough to be action-guiding and self-correcting, but always open to revision.

The shared lesson: in ethics as in measurement, the interesting failures live at the boundaries. The framework is most useful not when it gives a clean answer, but when it makes visible the tension between levels, the hidden externalities, and the feedback that is being suppressed.
