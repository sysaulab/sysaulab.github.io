An Essay on Coercion, Self-Image, and the Mediated Collective

The most dangerous war is the one that has already convinced itself it is a gift.

This essay is about the machinery of that conviction — the analogy between the individual who wraps domination in benevolence and the society that wages war while narrating liberation. It is about the media that runs the machinery, the reasons the lie keeps working, and the nested responsibility we owe to the levels we contain and the levels that contain us.

The analogy and its vertigo

Start with the image that will not leave: the rapist who convinces himself he is bringing joy. He does not experience himself as a predator. He experiences himself as a lover, a provider, a teacher — someone who knows what the other needs better than the other knows herself. The victim's resistance is read as confusion, ingratitude, or a failure to understand. The perpetrator's self-image is not a mask he wears for others. It is the story he tells himself to make his action livable.

Now scale the image. A society wages war. It does not experience itself as an aggressor. It experiences itself as a defender, a liberator, a civilizer, a bringer of democracy. The enemy's resistance is read as fanaticism, ingratitude, or a failure to understand its own interest. The society's self-image is not a mask worn for outsiders. It is the story curated by its media, its schools, its monuments, its leaders — the story that makes the action livable.

The pattern: domination wrapped in benevolence, the victim's voice excluded, "good" defined by the powerful, and self-deception that is not always cynical but is always convenient.

The analogy illuminates. It captures moral self-licensing, euphemism, dehumanization, and the way the perpetrator's framing dominates the story. It captures the fact that some perpetrators genuinely believe they are helping, others are cynical, and most are a mixture of both.

But the analogy must be broken as well as used. Rape is not about bringing joy; it is about power, control, humiliation, and entitlement. War is not a single act by a single mind; it is organized, sustained, institutional, and contested from within. Collapsing war and rape makes both less precise. The essay begins with the metaphor and spends its length earning the right to use it.

The question: How does a society come to see its own coercion as benevolence — and what role does media play in constructing the self that does the seeing?

The collective as a moral fiction that acts

Societies are not persons. But they are not fictions either. They have decision procedures — parliaments, commands, bureaucracies, elections, markets, media. They have persistent commitments — treaties, doctrines, laws, budgets, cultural memory. They have we-intentions: "we are liberating them," "we are civilizing," "we are defending civilization." They can act as group agents in a morally relevant sense.

The neuron analogy is tempting: if I murder someone and get the electric chair, dissenting cells feel the consequences. The whole is punished; the parts suffer. So too with a society under sanctions, war, or regime collapse. Causal entanglement is real.

But the analogy breaks at the moral level. Neurons are not moral agents with rights, voices, exit options, or duties. Citizens are. A dissenting citizen is not a dissenting cell. They can contest the narrative, resist, refuse, emigrate, leak, protest, or bear witness. Their dissent has moral standing in a way a neuron's does not.

The distinction: the collective can be responsible as a group, especially when its procedures produce and sustain harmful commitments. But responsibility is not distributed equally among members. It scales with participation, role, capacity to dissent, benefit, and knowledge.

Propaganda does not merely lie to outsiders. It curates the collective's self-image. It builds a cultural memory in which domination appears as gift, coercion as care, and the victim's voice as ingratitude or irrationality. This is collective self-deception institutionalized — not necessarily a single conscious liar, but a system that rewards reassuring narratives and punishes the ones that disturb the mirror.

So the collective is a real fiction: real in its effects, fictional in its unity. It acts, but not as a single mind. It is responsible, but not as a single culprit. It remembers, but through institutions that curate what is remembered.

Media as the organ of collective self-perception

Media is not a window on war. It is the apparatus by which the collective sees itself seeing. It supplies the vocabulary of selfhood — loyalty, cynicism, tribe, innocence — and it determines which deaths are grievable, which threats are existential, and which resistances are legible as aggression.

Three functions matter most.

Function One

Framing

Media decides what counts as a threat, who counts as a victim, and which violence is visible as violence. The same bombing is "pacification" or "terrorism" depending on who is describing it and for whom. Framing is not merely bias; it is the construction of the moral geography in which action becomes thinkable.

Function Two

Self-curation

Propaganda does not only lie to outsiders. It maintains the collective's self-image as defensive, humanitarian, civilizing. It supplies the reassuring narrative that makes coercion feel like care. This is not always a conscious conspiracy; it is often an institutional incentive: reassuring narratives sell, disturb the mirror and you lose access.

Function Three

Feedback distortion

Learning requires timely, accurate, causally coupled feedback. War media delivers delayed, diffuse, unattributable feedback. Lies are exposed years later; failures are blamed on "bad intelligence" or "the fog of war"; officials retire into commentary. The system is designed to prevent the correction that learning requires.

The modern twist is that the self is mediated all the way down. People do not first have a self and then consume media; the media ecology supplies the vocabulary of selfhood itself. War propaganda works because it offers an identity package, not just a threat claim. To doubt the narrative is to risk becoming the kind of person who doubts — disloyal, naive, or worse.

The mediated self: in the modern era, the self that goes to war — and the self that watches war — is constituted in part by the apparatus that describes it. The mirror is not passive. It shapes what it reflects.

Why the lie keeps working

Lies are a constant in most previous wars. Why do people continue to fall for them? The answer is not stupidity. It is structure.

Cause One

Threat psychology

Under perceived existential threat, people prioritize cohesion and certainty over accuracy. They rally around leaders, interpret ambiguity as evidence for the feared outcome, and punish internal dissent as betrayal. This is not a bug that education automatically fixes. It is a deep coalitional response, and propaganda activates it deliberately.

Cause Two

Identity-protective cognition

Believing the official narrative protects one's self-image ("my country does not start wars of aggression"), one's social belonging ("I am loyal"), and one's moral worldview ("we are the good guys"). Challenging the narrative can feel like challenging who one is. People are better at spotting propaganda by the other side than by their own.

Cause Three

Trust and intermediaries

Most people cannot verify intelligence, military movements, or diplomatic history. They rely on trusted intermediaries: government, media, experts, clergy, community leaders. If those intermediaries repeat the narrative — or if the trusted network is polarized — belief becomes a social shortcut, not a personal failure of intelligence.

Cause Four

Compliance is not belief

Some people doubt but stay silent. Some disbelieve but support "their side" instrumentally. Some go along because of career, family, community pressure, or fear. Propaganda does not need universal belief. It needs enough acquiescence and enough suppression of alternatives.

There is also a deeper reason. Learning from history is not a single act of memory. It requires independent verification, institutional memory that survives political turnover, accountability for official deception, education in propaganda techniques, protection for dissent, and post-conflict truth-telling rather than myth-making. Where those conditions are weak — which is most of the time — the default is not learning. The default is recurrence with new costumes.

The sharpest formulation: people do not fail to learn because they are ignorant of the past. They fail because the present is designed to feel exceptional, the costs of doubt are social, the feedback is delayed, and the institutions that might teach the lesson are often the ones with an interest in not teaching it.

The moral accounting

The evidence for deliberate pretexts in some major conflicts is strong, though uneven. The pattern is not a single unified conspiracy. It is institutional incentives plus elite narrative management, varying by case.

The Gulf of Tonkin incident in 1964 is the classic case: the August 4 attack never happened. Historians have concluded it was a false radar image, and the NSA historian concluded that officers deliberately falsified intercepted communications to make the attack appear real. Some scholars argue it was a genuine mistake; the most defensible reading is that the administration did not need to invent the incident from whole cloth but seized upon a dubious report to push through a resolution it had been preparing for.

Iraq is clearer. The Bush administration claimed Saddam Hussein possessed chemical and biological weapons. After the invasion, no such stockpiles were found. Colin Powell's UN presentation — including the infamous vial — was later exposed as based on a fabricated source and discredited intelligence. The Defense Intelligence Agency had warned internally that it lacked confirmed evidence of chemical weapons production, yet the public case went forward.

The pattern: policy first, pretext second, moral language third, cost displaced onto the vulnerable. Whether one calls it lying or cherry-picking to fit a pre-decided policy, the effect is a pretextual war.

War is also an agent of change — a furnace that can melt institutions and force rapid reorganization. It has expanded state capacity, accelerated citizenship rights, broken obsolete institutions, driven technological innovation, and triggered decolonization. But these are often byproducts, not purposes. The same furnace also produces authoritarian consolidation, ethnic cleansing, trauma, and destroyed social trust.

The moral error is to evaluate war's benefits while disregarding its price. Mass death, disability, trauma, and destroyed communities are not externalities to be bracketed; they are the thing war does. A reform purchased by immense suffering is not justified merely because some good later appeared. That is retrospective moral luck — crediting the outcome while erasing the victims.

The distinctions that matter: catalyst is not justification. Byproduct is not purpose. Outcome is not redemption of the price. Disregarding the price is not moral analysis; it is moral abstraction from the victims.

If actors deliberately shape conditions to provoke war because they expect beneficial reforms, that is not war acting as a catalyst. That is using war as an instrument, with all the moral and legal weight of aggression and conspiracy. The fact that the instrument sometimes produces side benefits does not sanitize the intent. It makes the actors responsible for the foreseeable costs, not just the hoped-for gains.

The reflexive turn: nested responsibility

The essay must implicate itself. It is written inside the media ecology it describes. It risks reproducing war's grammar — the clean narrative, the righteous distance, the victim as illustration. The reflexive turn is not a confession; it is a discipline.

What replaces the benevolent-war narrative is not cynicism. It is nested responsibility: the recognition that every level of complexity exists in mutual dependence with the levels it contains and the levels that contain it. Higher levels must preserve the integrity, agency, and conditions of lower levels. Lower levels must preserve the conditions and integrity of higher levels. No level may treat another merely as a resource.

The two failure modes: tyranny of the whole over the parts — the whole exploits or sacrifices its parts. And cancer of the parts over the whole — parts optimize themselves by destroying the larger system. Nested responsibility holds the tension between them.

This is not a moral algorithm. It does not tell you what to do in every case. It tells you what to check. It tells you which questions must be answered before an action can be called healthy, and which failure modes must be ruled out.

Who defined the good?
Who bears the price?
Whose voice is excluded?
Does the justification survive the evidence?

These four questions are not a formula. They are a discipline of attention. They apply to the war narrative, to the media that carries it, and to the self that receives it — including the self writing this essay. The essay ends not with a verdict on war but with a practice: suspicion turned inward, not as cynicism, but as the refusal to let the mirror go unexamined.

The shared lesson: in war as in ethics, the interesting failures live at the boundaries. The framework is most useful not when it gives a clean answer, but when it makes visible the tension between levels, the hidden externalities, and the feedback that is being suppressed.

The most dangerous war is the one that has already convinced itself it is a gift. The task is not to become paralyzed by that knowledge. The task is to build the habits — of verification, of dissent, of attention to the excluded voice — that make the conviction harder to sustain. That is nested responsibility in practice. It is the opposite of the benevolent war.
